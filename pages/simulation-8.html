Simulation 8

Abdominal Injury

Instructor Simulation Summary

An athlete is injured during a lacrosse game. Health care providers (HCPs) will decide if or when the athlete can return to play (RTP).

Instructor-Only Information

This simulation contains two cases. In one case, the player was injured on the right side (liver); in the other case, the athlete was injured on the left (spleen). While this aspect should be quick and easy to diagnose, the simulation is designed to promote a high index of suspicion and to monitor a patient or use appropriate tests to prevent putting an athlete in danger. Abdominal injuries can be slow to appear; thus, holding an athlete out of play is sometimes the best option so that a patient trend can be assessed. Both cases have a return-to-play (RTP) component.

Scenario Synopsis

A lacrosse athlete was diving across the goal to shoot and landed on another player’s bent knee. The injury is severe enough that the player is slow to get up and walks off the field.

Content Area

Students must be familiar with abdominal-related conditions and be able to perform tests or assessments that may rule in or rule out an abdominal or related condition.

Learning Objectives

  1. Use appropriate physical examination techniques for the abdomen, including palpation, percussion, auscultation, inspection, or others
  2. Choose specific tests or notice specific signs for specific conditions
  3. Identify the affected body systems or organs to refine a differential diagnosis
  4. Identify emergent conditions
  5. Use time appropriately to follow up on a condition if an outcome is uncertain
  6. Understand when to refer or create a plan of next steps for each patient

HCP Prebrief

This simulation will last up to 12 minutes.

This case will involve a sideline evaluation with a RTP decision. You may choose to reevaluate your patient before making a RTP decision, or you may return them to play after your initial evaluation.

You are allowed to speed up time. For example, you can state, “I would like to wait five minutes and then . . .” The proctor will speed up the case by five minutes, but you will be required to verbally account for your actions during the sped-up time. The appropriate use of this time is part of the simulation and rubric.

When you have successfully addressed the injury and treated the patient, you can leave; you do not need to use all 12 minutes. If you run out of time, you will be told, “Time is up.” You must stop where you are and leave the simulation area.

You may be asked to answer clarifying questions. This should not be interpreted as poor performance. Clarifying questions may be asked for many reasons.

Please return your equipment to the starting area where you received it.

Please be sure all equipment is returned to its original state or replaced, if needed.

Treat this person as a real patient. You should provide care as you would in an actual situation. If you are unclear, you can always ask the proctor.

If there is a piece of equipment that you need and it is not available, you can verbalize that piece of equipment and its use.

SAFETY: If any person says “Time-out; time-out,” please stop actions immediately and as safely as possible. Unless you can see that the SP needs assistance, you should immediately leave the area and find a proctor.

If you have any questions, please speak to your instructor.

Debrief

Please select one of the provided debriefing methods from the Introduction. It is important to have a whole-class debriefing session following each simulation.

Simulation Variations and Author Notes

This simulation could be run with any type of sport.

Instructors may also choose to include a patient who sustained a similar blow but did not have any internal organ injuries, so students can perform the same evaluation and determine which patient is clear to RTP and which needs to be reevaluated.

Abdominal cavity injuries can be very difficult to discover, and signs and symptoms are not always clear or present. A good history and a trend are very important in discovery. The SPs in these scenarios react more and may express more symptoms than in a real patient. This is intended to assist the HCP in discovering the condition and practicing the skills, but HCPs should be informed that an extreme index of suspicion and caution may be necessary to discover these on a real patient. Many cases can be referred to for spleen and liver injuries where signs and symptoms were ignored, not discovered, or were not indicative, and often, tragedy resulted.
