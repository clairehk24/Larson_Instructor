Simulation 13

Shoulder Injury

Instructor Simulation Summary

The purpose of this simulation is for health care providers (HCPs) to demonstrate the ability to evaluate an acute upper-extremity injury and apply immediate first aid and management for other possible injuries.

Instructor-Only Information

This simulation involves the emergent evaluation and management of a clavicle fracture.

Scenario Synopsis

There is a lacrosse tournament going on with four fields and one athletic trainer providing central coverage for all teams. During a lacrosse game, a player was hit from the left and fell onto their right shoulder. The lacrosse stick was trapped and jabbed the player in the neck and throat area. The player felt immediate pain. The patient has hoarseness, shortness of breath, and paresthesia in the right arm. The patient is unable to lift the right arm without severe pain.

Content Area

Students should have emergency care knowledge: Beginning level knowledge and experience with splinting, fractures, dislocations, upper-extremity anatomy, and management of respiratory compromise.

Learning Objectives

  1. Identify a differential diagnosis concurrent with the injury presentation
  2. Perform timely referral of the patient to definitive care consistent with their signs and symptoms
  3. Perform a continued examination of vital signs and patient condition throughout the duration of the examination
  4. Competently stabilize and immobilize fractures if indicated
  5. Recognize potential life or limb risks and rule out, discover, or manage emergencies related to this injury

CAATE 2020 Standards

  • Standard 70
  • Standard 71
  • Standard 78

HCP Prebrief

This simulation will take up to 10 minutes. You are tasked with assessing and treating all conditions found.

When you have successfully addressed the injury and treated the patient, you can leave. If you do not complete within 10 minutes, you will be told, “Time is up.” You must stop where you are and leave the simulation area. The proctor may ask, “Anything you would like to add?” and may ask clarifying questions.

You may be asked to clarify a differential diagnosis or other criteria. This should not be interpreted as doing good or bad. These are questions that may be asked of everyone to help clarify.

Please return your equipment to the starting area where you received it. Also, please be sure all equipment is returned to its original state or replaced, if needed. If something is broken, please inform the simulation assistant when you return it, or go into the supply room or cabinet and replace it.

Treat this person as a real patient. You should provide care as you would in an actual situation. If you are unclear, you can always ask the proctor.

Your vital sign equipment will be near the injury location. If you decide you need it, you can go and get it. Please replace this equipment when finished. If there is a piece of equipment you need and it is unavailable, you can verbalize that piece of equipment and its use.

For this simulation, the SP may refer to a note card or other document for information. This is not part of the simulation. If you have questions about this, you can always ask the patient or proctor.

SAFETY: If any person says “Time-out; time-out,” please stop actions immediately and as safely as possible. Unless you can see that the SP needs assistance, you should immediately leave the area and find a proctor.

If you have any questions, please speak to your instructor.

Debrief

Please select one of the provided debriefing method from the Introduction. It is important to have a whole-class debriefing session following each simulation.

Since this simulation involves students needing to connect technical skills to a real-world encounter, the Diamond debriefing method is recommended.

Simulation Variations and Author Notes

This scenario could involve the HCP running onto the field to evaluate the injured player. This could also be a high-stakes simulation encounter where the student is pressured to make a quick decision and move the patient off the field. In the past, we have also used older patients who played a different sport in the Senior Olympic Games or other such events. This provides students with an experience with an older patient, which they do not often get in traditional clinical rotations.
