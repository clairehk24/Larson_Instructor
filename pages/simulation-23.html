Simulation 23

Exertional Heat Stroke

Instructor Simulation Summary

Health care providers (HCPs) will evaluate, diagnose, and treat a runner with exertional heat stroke (EHS). Diagnosis will include the use of rectal thermometry, and treatment will include cold water immersion (CWI).

Scenario Synopsis

The patient is a future collegiate cross country runner for the school where you are an athletic trainer (AT), but the cross-country season has not yet begun. The runner is competing in a community 10K race two weeks before school starts and begins to experience signs and symptoms of EHS.

Content Area

Students should have beginning-level knowledge and skill practice with rectal thermometry, general patient assessment and history, EHS recognition, and management with CWI.

Learning Objectives

  1. Interview a patient on their pertinent medical history
  2. Identify signs and symptoms of EHS
  3. Implement emergency action procedures for EHS, including taking a rectal temperature
  4. Manage a patient with EHS symptoms, including actively cooling them to appropriate core body temperature with CWI until transfer of care arrives
  5. Record patient encounter in the electronic medical record (EMR) and provide patient education information on EHS and recovery

CAATE 2020 Standards

  • Standard 56
  • Standard 64
  • Standard 71

HCP Prebrief

This is a safe learning environment where you are free to make mistakes and ask questions. This simulation is divided into two parts. Each part will last up to 15 minutes. You are tasked with assessing and treating the patient for potential heat illness.

You will have up to 30 minutes to complete a focused history, physical examination, and treat this patient.

Station 1: The proctor will direct you to different stations during this encounter. One station will begin when you meet the patient until you complete the rectal thermometry procedure.

Station 2: The proctor will direct you to an area with other providers, and you will now manage and treat your patient based on the rectal temperature and any other presenting or history information you obtained.

After station 2: You will be done with the simulation.

At each station, when you have successfully and appropriately addressed the condition and treated the patient, you can leave. If you do not complete within 15 minutes, you will be told, “Time is up.” You must stop where you are and leave the simulation area. The proctor may ask, “Is there anything you would like to add?” and may ask clarifying questions.

You will be tasked with taking a rectal temperature and cooling a patient if needed.

All of your equipment is in each room.

Treat this person as a real patient. You should provide care as you would in an actual situation. If you are unclear, you can always ask the proctor.

Because this is an actual medical procedure, if you need to pause, you can say to the proctor, “I need a pause.” You will be given a moment, and you can ask the proctor a question at this time, if needed.

SAFETY: If any person says “Time-out; time-out,” please stop actions immediately and as safely as possible. Unless you can see that the SP needs assistance, you should immediately leave the area and find a proctor.

If you have any questions, please speak to your instructor.

Debrief

Please select one of the provided debriefing methods from the Introduction. It is important to have a whole-class debriefing session following each simulation.

Students participating in this simulation may benefit from utilizing the Diamond debriefing method because much of the discussion will likely lie outside of technical skills.

Simulation Variations and Author Notes

This scenario could be held on a collegiate cross-country course, and the runner or patient could be participating in a collegiate race. This scenario could also occur in another state with a high risk of EHS, such as the Midwest and South, with very humid, hot environments in late summer.

Due to the high-stress nature of this encounter, we strongly recommend that this be utilized as a formative (teaching) opportunity for students rather than an assessment simulation. This will lower students’ anxiety and allow them the freedom to ask questions and learn from this simulation.
