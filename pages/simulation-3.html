Simulation 3

Emergency Action Plan

Instructor Simulation Summary

As a group, students will review and consider emergency action plans (EAPs) specifically in reference to three conditions: sudden cardiac arrest (SCA), spinal motion restriction (SMR) with equipment removal, and life-threatening bleeding. Each person will contribute to the creation and review of the EAP in a specific role identified in the EAP.

Instructor-Only Information

This is a surprise simulation involving cardiac arrest (CA). The students are informed that they are preparing emergency action plans (EAPs) for a future simulation. However, after a short interaction about the EAP they reviewed or created, they will visit the location the EAP was created for, and it will be an actual CA situation where they will need to perform cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR). The groups for this simulation should be assigned by the instructor at least one week prior to the start of class. Students are informed in the Scenario Synopsis that the instructor may provide EAPs for review and editing, a template, or no guiding materials. Finally, you can choose to assign roles either prior to or at the start of the class. In the past, I have assigned the roles when I assign the groups.

Scenario Synopsis

In groups of three to six, health care providers (HCPs) will review an EAP or create an EAP. The EAP will address three situations: SCA, SMR with equipment removal, and life-threatening bleeding. This is intended to prepare the HCP for a future simulation or situation. Each student will be assigned a specific role and will discuss and review the EAP and conditions from that perspective. As a group, the EAP will be discussed for appropriateness, effectiveness, and clarity, and it will also be reviewed with equipment and in the actual spaces: pool, field, classroom, and stadium. Please speak to the instructor to clarify the EAP review or creation process for these situations.

Content Area

HCPs should have beginning to advanced knowledge of EAPs, knowledge of cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) (BLS certified), knowledge of life-threatening bleeding, and knowledge of SMR with equipment removal.

Learning Objectives

  1. Write and evaluate the guidelines for the management, equipment, and roles for SCA, SMR with equipment removal, and life-threatening bleeding in an EAP
  2. Consider and discuss the EAP from a specific vantage point of a head athletic trainer (AT), coach, administration, assistant AT, and so on
  3. Evaluate and discuss other complicating factors to providing care during SCA, SMR with equipment removal, and life-threatening bleeding, like difficult locations or access, equipment location and usage, pool or water areas, and other circumstances
  4. Rewrite the EAP for these conditions as needed, particularly focusing on communication, roles, and teamwork

  • Standard 70
  • Standard 71
  • Standard 88
  • Standard 92

HCP Prebrief

[As a reminder, no patient information should be provided to students in advance.]

Today, we are going to prepare for a future simulation by reviewing the EAPs each group created. We want to assess their effectiveness, determine whether they would help in an emergency, understand personnel roles, and identify or critique any missing elements. During today’s activity, you will edit, mark up, and make comments on the EAP situations you were assigned. Please wear the provided nametags to identify your role during our discussions: Head AT, assistant AT, intern, coach, AT student, administration, AT. A mentor is assigned to each group who will listen and assist with your discussion. They will try to guide you and ask questions that allow you to make the best decision about the EAP. They will not give you answers. Please be sure to not only view and discuss the EAP from your role as a future AT but also from the role you have been assigned. When the mentor is ready, they will take you to your location to specifically discuss how the EAP will work there.

SAFETY: During simulations, the phrase “Time-out; time-out” is used to stop actions immediately and as safely as possible. This is for safety. This phrase can also be used or placed within the EAP to signal a need to halt actions by one member.

Debrief

Please select one of the provided debriefing methods from the Introduction. It is important to have a whole-class debriefing session following each simulation.

An important topic to discuss during the debrief is how this might look different when only one or two HCPs are available or notice the arrest. Remind students that this is more likely the case for most sport. How do they get more help? What are the strategies for a more successful resuscitation? Is this covered in the EAP? Since this simulation is a surprise for the students and they are providing emergency care to patients, it may be useful to debrief using the Trauma-informed Psychologically Safe (TiPS) debriefing framework if it was stressful. However, this is rarely stressful for students, and any debriefing framework works well.

Postsimulation Activities

The students have not been provided with any information on a debrief or postsimulation activities. They have no information that suggests they would be doing a simulation on this day. These downloads are provided to instructors only. If you would like the students to complete these activities, please distribute them to the students after the simulation. If the simulation is not a surprise, they can be released in advance.

Simulation Variations and Author Notes

To my surprise, students love this simulation. I was concerned about the deception and surprise aspect, and the weight of someone dying, but most students suggest I do it every year. Many students would like to see it be more difficult or more surprising.

Deception has been used in this simulation because it is believed that CA is one of those conditions that can happen anytime, anywhere, and the HCP must always be ready for this. Additionally, it is both less and more stressful to operate in a team, but this practice is essential in getting things done well.

Why does EMS stop CPR to check a pulse and breathing? There are two reasons for this. It is actually quite difficult to find a pulse on someone who is near dead (or dead). The EMS provider should feel for a pulse while CPR is occurring (because you can feel it) and then ask the HCP to stop and notice it disappear. Second, just like any transfer of care, the new provider should ensure they understand and verify the patient’s condition.

Variations

  1. A great variation is to have a bystander, family member, coach, athlete, or other person as part of the simulation.
  2. Another great variation is to have a few certified ATs participate. They can know they will be participating or will be nearby, but they have to stay in their role. Doing this as a team with people of all levels is a great experience. You could also ask a team doctor, resident, or HCP to be available.
  3. If EMS personnel arrive, the rescue could be a team effort, and transfer of care could be practiced.
  4. For the EAP class, for more advanced students, or students with more knowledge of EAPs, you can have them write EAPs for specific locations rather than locating already created EAPs.
  5. Most ATs will be managing sport alone or with one other provider. You can conduct this simulation with a group of two, who will then need to contact additional help (e.g., call other staff, use a coach, etc.) or perform resuscitation alone. This can be done in rotation so that the first two HCPs use an instructor or mentor, and then those HCPs become additional help for the next simulation group.
  6. This could be repeated, perhaps without the surprise. This extra rehearsal of the EAP allows students to understand what aspects are relevant or important in an EAP and to change them for this second effort. This will allow them to practice their suggestions.
  7. Students prepare for life-threatening bleeding during this simulation, but it does not appear in this simulation. I usually do a quick simulation for life-threatening bleeding on a different day (see Author Notes and Life-Threatening Bleeding simulation).
