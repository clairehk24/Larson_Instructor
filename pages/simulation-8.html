Simulation 8

Abdominal Injury

Instructor Simulation Summary

An athlete is injured during a lacrosse game. Health care providers (HCPs) will decide if or when the athlete can return to play (RTP).

Instructor-Only Information

This simulation contains two cases. In one case, the player was injured on the right side (liver); in the other case, the athlete was injured on the left (spleen). While this aspect should be quick and easy to diagnose, the simulation is designed to promote a high index of suspicion and to monitor a patient or use appropriate tests to prevent putting an athlete in danger. Abdominal injuries can be slow to appear; thus, holding an athlete out of play is sometimes the best option so that a patient trend can be assessed. Both cases have a return-to-play (RTP) component.

Scenario Synopsis

A lacrosse athlete was diving across the goal to shoot and landed on another player’s bent knee. The injury is severe enough that the player is slow to get up and walks off the field.

Content Area

Students must be familiar with abdominal-related conditions and be able to perform tests or assessments that may rule in or rule out an abdominal or related condition.

Learning Objectives

Use appropriate physical examination techniques for the abdomen, including palpation, percussion, auscultation, inspection, or others

Choose specific tests or notice specific signs for specific conditions

Identify the affected body systems or organs to refine a differential diagnosis

Identify emergent conditions

Use time appropriately to follow up on a condition if an outcome is uncertain

Understand when to refer or create a plan of next steps for each patient

Simulation Variations and Author Notes

This simulation could be run with any type of sport.

Instructors may also choose to include a patient who sustained a similar blow but did not have any internal organ injuries, so students can perform the same evaluation and determine which patient is clear to RTP and which needs to be reevaluated.

Abdominal cavity injuries can be very difficult to discover, and signs and symptoms are not always clear or present. A good history and a trend are very important in discovery. The SPs in these scenarios react more and may express more symptoms than in a real patient. This is intended to assist the HCP in discovering the condition and practicing the skills, but HCPs should be informed that an extreme index of suspicion and caution may be necessary to discover these on a real patient. Many cases can be referred to for spleen and liver injuries where signs and symptoms were ignored, not discovered, or were not indicative, and often, tragedy resulted.
