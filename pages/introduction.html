Welcome to the world of simulation education! We are delighted you have chosen to incorporate simulation as a teaching tool into your curriculum. This introduction will provide basic information on simulation but will mostly focus on how to administer the simulations in Clinical Simulations for the Athletic Trainer. It should be noted that these simulations are written from the perspective of educators who have used simulation in graduate education programs for years. While some items may be referenced so that you can review important evidence, other information may be based on the experience or opinions of the authors. As such, this introduction is not meant to be a review of literature or an in-depth education on simulation but rather a guide to the 32 simulations provided. Undoubtedly, your students and your experience will be different than ours. For better or worse, that is the nature of health care and education, always changing and always locally influenced. In the end, most students find the simulation experience intimidating but self-confidence-building, nerve-racking yet rewarding, and difficult while educational.

We hope you enjoy the journey of incorporating simulation into your educational experiences and can weather the same emotional experience as your students. Conducting simulations is just as intimidating, nerve-racking, and difficult for those who administer the simulations as for those who participate, but thankfully, it is also just as self-confidence-building, rewarding, and educational for the facilitators.

What Is Simulation?

Simulation is the creation or recreation of an experience for skill practice, problem solving, education, experience, or assessment.1 Specifically, simulation is a tool used to provide an opportunity for learners to experience something or demonstrate knowledge.

The role of simulations in education and health care was pioneered and strongly developed by the field of nursing and the International Nursing Association for Clinical Simulation and Learning (INACSL). The INACSL is a great resource for nearly any question related to simulations. Their publication Healthcare Simulation Standards of Best PracticeTM Evaluation of Learning and Performance2 is an excellent resource we recommend.

Preparation Focused Simulation

In Clinical Simulations for the Athletic Trainer, each simulation contains preparatory activities for students, so they arrive at the simulation with the opportunity to know, practice, or demonstrate what is required of them. When these preparatory experiences are provided, the students get to demonstrate what they know. By contrast, a “Gotcha” simulation mostly demonstrates what students do not know. Being surprised or fearful is not an ideal condition for learning or performance, and it often creates a negative experience that may persist into future interactions. For this reason, the authors do not generally endorse “Gotcha” simulations. There is one exception to this rule: Simulation 3: Emergency Action Plan. In this simulation, students are told to prepare or review an emergency action plan (EAP), including how cardiac arrest should be covered in the EAP and to prepare to discuss and evaluate their EAP choices with an athletic trainer during class. In reality, they will encounter a cardiac arrest during this simulation and must work as a team to provide care for the patient. However, it is still far from a “Gotcha” simulation as the students received preparation activities related to cardiac arrest and emergencies in advance of the simulation, so they had the opportunity to be well prepared, without being told exactly what was planned. Additionally, it seems particularly appropriate that sudden cardiac arrest is a surprise simulation because it replicates something important about cardiac arrest events: They are a surprise.

Simulations can be delivered in varying modes. These could include low-fidelity task trainers to high-fidelity, full-body, computerized task trainers that sweat and cry and do much more. Simulation can also be delivered using virtual reality, tabletop discussions, video screens, or many other formats. In Clinical Simulations for the Athletic Trainer, the primary mode of delivery employs people to portray patients, known as Standardized Patients (SPs).

Standardized Patient (SP) and Embedded Personnel (EP)

An SP is a person trained to consistently portray signs and symptoms of a specific injury or illness.3 Standardized patients have historically been used to teach and assess clinical skills in medical education.4 Previous literature has shown SPs provide students with realistic experiences to supplement direct patient interaction.5 In recent years, athletic training programs have found SPs provide consistency when evaluating clinical skills and can provide immediate feedback for students to improve.6,7

SPs are also helpful for improving athletic training students’ interpersonal and clinical skills.8 The opportunity for students to practice clinician-to-patient communication in a low-stakes environment is very beneficial for enhancing students’ self-confidence.8-10 Due to immediate feedback that the SP can provide, this teaching tool also promotes critical reflection in athletic training students that may improve their skills in the future.9

SPs are found to be reliable and objective for assessing athletic training students' clinical proficiency in orthopedic physical exams, concussion evaluation, and general medical encounters.11 Additionally, students can practice critical psychosocial evaluation and referral skills with an SP.12 These are sensitive proficiencies that are ideal for practicing in a low-stakes environment before interacting with a live patient. Lastly, and most importantly, students can translate their skills with SPs to their future clinical practice.13 Research supports the notion that skills practiced or learned in simulation translate to clinical practice, where research in nursing has demonstrated that simulations are as effective as clinical experiences in up to 50 percent of a student’s clinical time.13-15

For each simulation where SPs are required, downloadable information is available for the SPs to memorize. The SPs should be given ample time to learn and rehearse their role before portraying it for your students. SPs can be hired by you, and many institutions have SPs on campus for other medical education, or you may find or create academic classes for SPs. In general, when working with an SP for a simulation, we have scheduled the practice and rehearsal as follows.

After confirming an SP for a simulation, a script or case is sent to the SP, and a 15-minute follow-up meeting is scheduled. Prior to the meeting, the SP is expected to read the script and outline any questions or concerns, verify the case is understood, outline specific or important details, and verify content and expectations. After this, the SP meets with a facilitator for a 30-minute rehearsal. Generally, the case is practiced at least twice during this rehearsal. One time, the simulation is done with a health care provider (HCP) providing care as might be expected or as desired. The other time, the facilitator or person portraying the HCP for the rehearsal will perform the case with some errors or by including anticipated questions or problems. This is to ensure the SP is ready for all types of interactions and care. It is recommended that the SP be paid for this scheduled simulation time plus an additional hour of self-practice (0.25 time to initially read the case, 0.25 to discuss questions, 0.5 for memorization or practice before and after rehearsal), as well as the scheduled simulation time, time for providing feedback, and, if applicable, debriefing time.

SPs are not always just patients. SPs participate in the evaluation of the HCP by completing evaluations, providing viewpoints at the debrief, or providing direct feedback to students. While you may not always be able to have SPs at the debriefing, you can utilize the feedback they provide to share with students during the debrief. In the simulations we have provided, the practice of providing feedback has not been specifically outlined. However, it is a model practice that is easy to incorporate and use. SPs provide an excellent perspective for how something felt, or how it might be interpreted by a patient. Please include SP feedback in your simulation feedback, debrief, or evaluations as you see fit. At a minimum, it is important to ask the SPs if they have any feedback about the experience. This is often interesting or even surprising but always valuable information to provide to the students.

We have sometimes suggested that an SP and a proctor be the same person. Even though a proctor is portraying the patient, like in Simulation 25: Foot Pain Telehealth, the SP training should still follow the format of the SP and be adequate enough to reliably portray the patient and evaluate the HCP. Similarly, some cases have an EP. This is a person who is not a patient but is considered an accomplice or is in cooperation with the simulation or SP. This EP may be a parent asking about their child, like in Simulation 18: CSI, or a coach being educated about a patient’s condition, like in Simulation 9: Coach Education. For simulations that use an EP, the SP may or may not be present, like in Simulation 7: Phases of Healing, where the HCPs and the EP only have documentation of the patient’s injury and no actual patient is present. The EP is simply another simulation participant who is not the SP. These individuals are likely to need the same amount and structure of rehearsal as the SP as well as the same compensation.

Simulation Assessments

The first thing we tell the students about their role in a simulation is, “You are now an HCP. You do not have the luxury of giving up, saying you don’t understand, or telling the patient you don’t know what to do next (unless that is the kind of provider you want to be). Everyone in this class is a colleague. We are all going to work together to become the HCP we have imagined or know we want to be. All simulations, all feedback, and all assessments are done within this context. You will decide what the appropriate care or decision is. You will discuss among your colleagues what could have been better or what was exemplary.” The idea that everyone has already made it, and we are all just practicing medicine, is very helpful when it comes to evaluation and assessment for simulation.

We recommend that all the students be evaluated for each simulation in some form or fashion. Over the years, two things have rung true when evaluating simulations. The students want feedback. They want clear, abundant, helpful, and timely feedback, but, as you will see when you debrief and evaluate, feedback is most effective when it is done as if the students are colleagues. Try to provide students the time and space to consider their actions or the actions of others, to discuss the pros and cons amongst themselves, and to come to conclusions about the experience. They usually make the same discovery and recognize what you observed. As an HCP, there are many instances where the “correct” answer is on a spectrum of what is best or appropriate in a given situation. These simulations should be no different. The simulations here do not exist in a space where every correct approach or wrong decision is clear and easy. To start on the right foot, we recommend that all students read the introduction found in the ebook and the students’ HKPropel Access and complete an assignment from Thanks for the Feedback.16 This book provides an excellent perspective on how to handle feedback, especially when it is difficult. It can be hard to remember how much HCPs put themselves out there to be critiqued, analyzed, poked, and molded. This is exhausting work, and it says nothing of the emotions involved in providing care to individuals and how that affects a person. In general, it is our belief that feedback is best integrated when it occurs in a manner that recognizes that we are all educated, we are all trying, we all want good care, and we all want to succeed. The rubrics, evaluations, and debriefs in Clinical Simulations for the Athletic Trainer are written with this in mind.

All the simulations in Clinical Simulations for the Athletic Trainer provide a rubric and some level of evaluation. However, these are only templates, and we have purposely created numerous variations across the simulations. One variation that we often integrate into the evaluations is a critical repeat (CR) criterion. This is something that is essential about the simulation, which if not achieved, would mean that the point of the simulation or the minimum standard of care was missed. It is sometimes a skill, like performing adequate CPR, calling emergency medical services (EMS), or taking a blood sugar, but could also be a score of 0 in any category or an overall score lower than 70 percent. Finding a balance where the HCP prepares and approaches the situation in a professional manner, while still meeting the bare minimum care expectations, is important. To achieve this balance, we sometimes use this CR. Sometimes this CR is chosen by the instructor, and sometimes the students decide on this criterion. This provides some stakes in the simulation but is still low stakes without having any stakes. As much as students enjoy the simulations, they rarely want to repeat them. If a student misses this criterion, they have to repeat the simulation in the future.

Using simulation in education can serve a variety of purposes in an athletic training program. A program may choose any or all forms of simulation assessment to meet its programmatic needs. Formative simulations are useful as a low-stakes skills check for students and can provide realistic scenarios for students to practice a crucial lifesaving skill without the pressure of making a mistake on an actual patient. These types of simulation assessment may be graded, but consider grading on a different scale (e.g., 80 percent or better is A, 70 percent to 80 percent B, <70 percent = CR). The simulation could also be worth fewer points so that students can use these simulations as learning experiences to improve their clinical skills without the risk of negatively affecting their grade. Consider raising the stakes on the simulation but lowering the stakes on the negative consequences for their performance. We can assure you students will try harder and succeed more often when the penalties are lower. Also, when students are required to repeat a simulation, they are always grateful in the end. They really solidify their understanding after that repetition.

As much as we have discussed formative assessments, we use summative assessments as well. A summative assessment can be used not only to prove to the program or course that an objective was met but also to reinforce concepts already learned, provide self-confidence, practice, and may even be a moment for leaders to come forth and demonstrate a bit of mastery. The simulation is a time to shine and hopefully do their best that day.

Summative simulations are typically implemented as a final evaluation at the end of a term to evaluate how well students achieved the course learning outcomes. These simulations are often more holistic around the entire patient care process. Objective Structured Clinical Examinations (OSCEs) are implemented by many health professions programs as a reliable form of assessment because they have predetermined criteria and use SPs who are able to provide beneficial feedback.17 These simulations are inherently more stressful on a student, which is why implementing several formative simulations throughout the term is useful to help prepare a student physically and mentally for the summative simulation.

Everything Is Not Real

It is important to note that a simulation does not always intend or aspire to create or recreate an entirely realistic experience. For instance, if an athlete wants to improve at batting, it would not be productive to have the athlete play more games or to have a pitcher throw 300 fastballs, especially if the athlete is not very experienced, skilled, and knowledgeable at batting. It would likely be more effective to spend the same amount of time recreating, enhancing, or isolating specific components like the noise, distractions, heat, or practicing timing; hand placement; swing; or other variables either as a unit or broken into different experiences to improve the athlete’s batting. In this same way, the simulations contained in Clinical Simulations for the Athletic Trainer do not always create, replicate, or attempt to be real in each instance. Isolating, or approximating things, is not only sufficient, sometimes it is preferred so the focus remains where it is needed. A student demonstrating spinal motion restriction on the lawn just outside the classroom is usually preferred to doing this skill in the football stadium. Being in the stadium may hinder the experience. The student is likely to be overwhelmed by too much emotion, too many noises, too high stakes, too many new variables, and not being entirely sure of the required skills or abilities needed. Being forced to perform under those circumstances generally creates a negative and confusing experience for most learners. This does not create a learning environment but rather a performance environment. Most of the simulations in Clinical Simulations for the Athletic Trainer are designed to provide the safety, time, and guidance necessary for learning.

Similarly, do not be intimidated or overwhelmed by feeling the need to make other components “real” unless there is a specific reason for it. As an example, creating a “real” looking fracture may not be necessary. While it may be wonderful to do and may be helpful, placing an arm band around the radius and ulna that says “deformity,” or “open fracture,” may be just as valuable or more valuable. The student knows what it is, where it is, and can now demonstrate their skill at patient evaluation and management in a distraction-free environment with a small zone of focus. Many students have expressed how this exact simulation practice, which recreated the high-stakes situation but simplified their expectations, was tremendously helpful when it actually happened to them during a clinical situation.

The Fiction Contract

Simulations usually include an agreement with the student called a fiction contract.18,19 The fiction contract is an agreement about the student expectations and the facilitator’s role in the simulation. This outlines an agreement and understanding for both student and facilitator that everything is as real as it can be in this created situation, and that everyone will treat it as real and act in a professional manner. The contract explicitly states that if the facilitator says something exists, then it exists. The contract will also outline the parameters for each participant’s privacy in a simulation. A student may experience something unexpected or do something unexpected, and while discussing it among others who participated or with the instructor is acceptable, it is not permissible to identify someone and to discuss the events publicly. Finally, the contract outlines the psychological safety of the simulation, reminds the student of their professional status, and the need to be professional. It should be noted that participating in a simulation can be emotional for students. Providing a space for them to experience these emotions and work through the process of appropriately expressing or managing the emotions is a natural part of providing care. This too may be covered in the contract. You can find examples of these online to use as a template.

Simulation Format

We have provided you with several different types of simulation formats to choose from. The basic formats are described here but can be combined. To further assist you with selecting an appropriate simulation for your students’ learning goals, we have created a Simulation Finder available for you to access from HKPropel.

One-on-one live patient encounter: These simulations are designed to involve a prescheduled one-on-one encounter with an SP or EP and one student (HCP) at a time. A proctor may be in the room, or some other evaluation method may be used.

Telehealth encounter: These simulations are designed to involve an encounter with an SP or EP and occur over the phone or via video-conferencing.

Tabletop simulations: A tabletop simulation is one where the whole class participates in the simulation all at once with a facilitator present. An SP may or may not be involved in these simulations.

Peer simulations: These simulations are designed to allow students to act as and rotate through the patient, HCP, and possibly proctor roles during the simulation.

Small group simulations: These simulations are similar to a one-on-one encounter in that they are prescheduled and include an SP or EP encounter. However, a group of students will provide care as a team. These differ from a tabletop simulation because each group has a separate scheduled time.

Standard List of Equipment

Most of the simulations require some amount of equipment. Having the same equipment, regardless of the situation, is helpful because it prevents the equipment from foreshadowing the event. For instance, an instructor may choose to always have a cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) mask, a reflex hammer, and a blood glucose monitor available, but these won’t always be used in every simulation. Similarly, a fanny pack or other rescue pack generally contains the same equipment for each event. Here, we have listed a sideline equipment list and a fanny pack equipment list. When a simulation lists a clinical setting, it is assumed that all equipment available in sports medicine clinic is available; however, some specifics may be listed.

Some simulations require a student to gather equipment based on the sport or situation, while others have it available. Deciding what to bring, how to organize a bag, and understanding the administrative costs and decisions to create a sideline, fanny, or other equipment bag are valuable learning or preparatory activities.

The two options, sideline and fanny, are chosen so that students must make choices that recreate their real environment. If a student runs onto the field to assess an athlete, they likely do not have a sphygmomanometer, stethoscope, thermometer, or glucometer in their fanny pack and cannot provide oxygen. So, if that equipment is required, it is on the sideline. Someone has to bring the needed equipment to the HCP, the athlete has to be moved, or the HCP has to get it and bring it back and loses valuable time if they are not prepared in advance. This is another small detail that mentally prepares students for actual situations where decisions must be made.

Debriefing Methods

A debriefing session is considered best practice following a simulation and is a key component in simulation-based education.5 Simulationists consider the debrief to be the most essential component of the simulation process for learning1 and is one of the most difficult to do well as a facilitator. One of INACSL’s best practice publications says it best: “Learning is dependent on the integration of experience and conscious consideration or reflection of the activity”.20(p27) The integration of the experience and conscious consideration of the simulation is the point of the debrief. It is a planned reflective moment that can occur in many different formats but is often accomplished through a guided meeting, written reflection, or conversation. For nearly all of the simulations in Clinical Simulations for the Athletic Trainer, we have imagined a classroom meeting that occurs sometime after the simulation. Some people prefer directly after, but others prefer to have the meeting at least 24 hours after the encounter. A debrief 24 hours after the encounter mimics actual clinical practice where a provider cannot step away and discuss what just occurred or where results of a treatment may not be known for 24 hours, or a patient does not return until the next day. In this instance, a provider may toss and turn at night, ask friends what they would have done, describe to partners what was experienced, and have some downtime to reflect prior to a debrief. Requiring the student to manage their response to their performance is a skill that providers must learn, and this can be their moment to practice it. This is another small way that simulations are much more than the moment of care and how they reflect or replicate the lives of providers.

Many of the postsimulation activities incorporate a specific reflection that is intended to prime students for a group discussion during the debrief. However, many students will complete the postsimulation activities after the debrief. Either way, these two work in concert for the student to reflect deeply on the experience. While the debrief should clarify and correct mistakes or “problems” that occurred during the simulation, it is the goal of the facilitator to set the stage for the students to reflect on the experience and to hopefully recognize and discuss the mistakes, problems, and differences in care and to promote the “licensed” HCP colleagues discussing their actions and choices among themselves with as little facilitator intervention as possible. The facilitator brings up the topics for the colleagues to discuss, fills in gaps in knowledge, but attempts to have the providers practice the exchange of learning and knowledge with their colleagues.

Regardless of the debriefing method or process chosen, before starting any debrief, it is often useful to use an activity to prime the students so they are prepared for a discussion. We start almost every debrief with one of two simple activities. One, have students write two things they did well on the front of an index card and two things they did not do well, want to improve upon, or have questions about. Depending on the debrief, you may start the debrief by asking students to share some of these in the beginning, have the students keep their note cards and use them during a moment in the discussion, or have them turn them in and only refer to them when needed or at the end. This not only primes them for the discussion, but it also calms some of the excited and anxious voices in their head. The other activity is to ask two questions of the group and let them respond however they prefer. While they do this, write important things to return to on a whiteboard or your notes. Question 1: “How did it go?” or “How did you feel?” Question 2: “What happened?” Question 1 clears the air and lets students get their emotions out. It just lets the students honestly express their anxiety, confusion, or elation. Question 2 helps to center and frame the conversation so that we are sure we are all talking about the same thing and understand the same event. While questions 1 and 2 are answered, we usually make notes on the whiteboard. These notes help during future discussions to point to the timeline or occurrences during the simulation and address unresolved anxieties or problems.

The most complete resource on debriefing is “Healthcare Simulation Standards of Best PracticeTM” by Decker et al.20 This resource outlines numerous methods of debriefing and four specific criteria that meet the standard for an effective debriefing process. Please read this guide and have it handy when considering the debrief. However, to get you started, we have outlined six debriefing methods and their role or unique characteristics. These are included in a table at the end of this section, and you can also find this table on HKPropel for handy reference as you plan each simulation. This is not an exhaustive list, but it will give you a place to start when selecting debriefing models.

Three-Phase Methods

There are several three-phase debriefing methods outlined by Abulebda et al.5 We will discuss a few of the three-phase debriefing methods (Diamond and Gather, Analyze, Summarize [GAS]) here.

Diamond

The Diamond debriefing involves three phases: description, analysis, and application.21 This type of debrief “is specifically designed to allow an exploration of the non-technical aspects of a simulated scenario”.21(p171) This technique is built in the shape of a diamond and walks the learners through the reflection and self-discovery process of starting with the raw details of what happened in the simulation, then discussing the emotions and feelings experienced, and finally closing with students identifying how they will apply their experience into the real world and future simulated experiences.21 The creators of this simulation practice stress the importance of the visual diamond as a reminder to not revert to technical aspects of the simulation but to stay focused on nontechnical skills.21 For example, in our experience, when students participate in the exertional heat stroke simulation (Simulation 23), they are so thoroughly prepared that many of the technical skills are not a large part of the discussion. Rather, they focus on how they felt, what made them nervous, how this is not a big deal anymore, and how to better prepare the patient and the provider for this procedure. For this simulation, the Diamond debrief method has been more suitable as there is little to discuss technically.

Gather, Analyze, Summarize

The GAS debriefing model is a simple, three-phase approach to debriefing postevent. Sawyer et al22 describe this process as three phases that are structured to guide students through reflection on an event, such as a simulation. The initial phase, Gather, involves the facilitator mostly listening to the students describe what happened and sharing how they felt about the event. Approximately half the time of the debriefing session should be spent on the second phase, Analyze. During this phase, the facilitator may provide feedback to the students on their performance and facilitate student self-reflection on their own actions and identify areas for improvement. Finally, the Summarize phase allows the facilitator to provide a student-focused summary of the debriefing session by asking students to reflect on the actions that were effective and identify specific areas for future improvement.

Plus-Delta

The plus-delta (+Delta) debriefing approach has been promoted as a method that is suitable for both inexperienced and experienced debriefers and for time-limited situations. The goal of this debriefing method is to promote learner self-assessment in an easy-to-follow format.23 Though a facilitator may pose or guide questions, learner self-assessment is crucial in this method. In many health care positions, the only person or the person who will most frequently assess whether the patient evaluation, care, or process was adequate, excellent, or insufficient, is the provider. So, allowing students to explore this evaluation process with one another sets them up for doing this in the future and in a safe and collegial environment that may be used in the future.

The +Delta format can be used as the debrief itself, or it can be used as students go through the phases of describing, analyzing, and refining, or it can be used as a tool while going through any debrief process. While the use of this method is flexible, we have mostly used it to engage students in self-evaluating and realizing the positive things that occurred during a simulation by asking questions about what worked or went well or what did students like or feel positive about. Generally, as students talk about this among themselves, they realize that their perception, usually of not doing so well, may not match their performance. Indeed, often how we feel, particularly in a new or stressful situation, does not match reality. This inquiry is important as it reframes what occurred and what was experienced, often in a much more positive and less emotional frame. After this reflection, we use the delta portion, or the change portion of the discussion, to explore if any changes are appropriate or would improve the provider or patient’s experience or outcome. We have found this method to be a great way to put students in a more positive frame of mind to explore their experience. It is also a very easy method to follow. While we rarely use the +Delta process in any other way, it is possible to ask both questions simultaneously: What went well and what would you change? This choice allows students to gravitate toward what is most pressing in their minds and to explore, back-and-forth, what most resonates for a student or group at that moment.

Reflective Practice

The Reflective Practice in Health Care and How to Reflect Effectively by Koshy et al24 appears in some of the postsimulation activities. The format chosen for this written reflection is the basic format and idea used in for this debriefing method. The following questions are asked of the students and adapted from the Koshy et al24 article. Please refer to the article for more specifics.

What happened, where did it occur and who was involved? Describe the situation.

How did you feel initially, during the interaction, and after?

What did you like about what you did or how the situation unfolded and what did you not like or what would change, improve, or need to refresh or learn more about?

Can you specifically identify how this will change your approach to health care, this situation, or this type of patient or condition in the future? What will you do differently now and in the future?

Can you describe how things will be different when you make these changes? You can start by describing how this changes you, your approach to health care, or how you manage patients. This can include prevention or changes to the field that improve this situation or your role in it.

PEARLS Debriefing Framework

The Promoting Excellence and Reflective Learning in Simulation (PEARLS) Debriefing tool was developed in 2015 by Dr. Eppich and Dr. Cheng.25 This debriefing tool offers a semi-structured framework for instructors to use as a guide during their debriefing session. The framework includes a printable card with each phase, the objective of that phase, the task, and transitional phrases for the instructor to utilize at the beginning of each phase. This framework offers a five-phase approach to debriefing and begins with the instructor setting the ground rules for the session (setting the scene).25 Next, students share their reactions and feelings the simulation elicited (reactions). The third phase allows students to describe what happened in the simulation (description).25 The fourth phase (analysis) allows the instructor to elaborate on specific performance domains.25 This phase is typically longer if the simulation involved multiple performance gaps to discuss.25 Finally, the fifth phase (application and summary) concludes with takeaways that are either instructor centered or learner centered, allowing the instructor to choose how they will close the debriefing session.25

Trauma-Informed Psychologically Safe (TiPS) Debriefing Framework

While we understand every simulation is fiction, some simulations are more real than others and likewise, some cause more realistic HCP emotional responses than others. As such, some of the simulations in Clinical Simulations for the Athletic Trainer may elicit psychological or emotional stress. Particularly the simulations that are a surprise to the students and involve life-threatening conditions like Simulation 3 (EAP) and Simulation 24 (Rotator Cuff Complication). Additionally, Simulation 14 (Taping Ethics) has scenarios which address emotional, physical, and sexual abuse. Similarly, Simulation 27 (General Medical Checks) and Simulation 30 (Breaking Bad News) includes topics that affect mental health. These topics may also evoke psychological or emotional stress on the students. An effective, trauma-informed debriefing structure may be most useful to you if you discover students are experiencing psychological or emotional stress during these, or any other simulation. Harder et al26 developed a debriefing framework specifically designed for tending to the emotional needs of students following a traumatic simulation such as patient death.

The TiPS debriefing framework consists of five phases (Orientation, Review, Catharsis, Psychoeducation, Recover) with a question prompt at the end of each phase to ensure students are ready to move on to the next phase.26 The first phase (Orientation) allows facilitators to establish group norms and set the tone for the debriefing session.26 This ensures a safe, judgment-free learning environment for students.26 Phase two, Review, encourages students to share a description of the events that occurred during the simulation.26 Phase three, Catharsis, focuses on the students’ emotional reactions to the simulation allowing them the space to reflect on and share the feelings they experienced.26 Psychoeducation is the fourth phase of the TiPS framework which focuses on normalizing the emotions students experienced and providing them with coping or self-care strategies.26 Finally, phase five, Recover, allows students to share the positive things they learned through the simulation, or through the debriefing session.26 This phase encourages students to think ahead to the future and apply these lessons to their future clinical practice.26 One final aspect to consider when choosing to utilize the TiPS framework is that it is designed to be conducted in small groups of 8 to 10 students.26 If your cohort of students is larger than this and you would like to utilize the TiPS framework, it may be best to divide your students up into smaller groups and have a trained facilitator in each group.

Please refer to the Debriefing Methods table for a quick reference on the six models we have just discussed.

While we have briefly outlined six debriefing models, there are many more in the simulation literature. We recommend every simulation be followed up by a debrief, and we encourage you to utilize a structured framework that best meets the needs of your students. As in real life, it is important to note that the debrief may not achieve all of its goals in the moment. If only we could have a debrief for everything and immediately walk away happy, more knowledgeable, and better. More likely, students will consider the discussions and activities over days and weeks, or it may arise when they experience the situation in real life. Do not be discouraged if everyone does not walk away immediately satiated by the debrief experience. It may take time and many other elements working together at the right time to produce the results and the clinicians we hope to see providing health care.

Simulation Class

The simulations in Clinical Simulations for the Athletic Trainer may be used as single events that are chosen by instructors for particular experiences or by programs to meet specific goals. However, many of these simulations were originally created as part of a semester-long class where students participated in 14 to 18 simulations over the semester and has been developed into a year-long experience for students.

While the method described here may not be a perfect model for a simulation course, it describes how this was handled and administered with great success. The schedule for the course had one day where the simulations occurred and one day for a debrief and preparation for the next simulation. The course was listed for 4 hours on the simulation day but the time frame was usually 2 to 2.5 hours, and a student only had to attend for their 10 to 15 minutes. The following day should be a minimum of 90 minutes, but 120 minutes is useful. On this second day, the simulation debrief occurs during the first portion of class and during the latter portion, students are provided preparatory information for the simulation that will occur the next week. Often during the second portion of the class, a lecture or other experiential learning will occur that specifically prepares the students to succeed at the presimulation activities and the next simulation. A previously used course outline has been included as an example. Obviously, the content is limited to the content taught to the students up to that moment in the program. However, depending on the focus of the simulation, students have started new material as part of the simulation or when it is taught concurrently with other courses. If content is new, the presimulation activities and preparation day are key to student success.

Students, faculty, and others have requested that simulations occur year-round and cover all topics from the educational program. This format is essentially the same as described earlier, but instead of the simulation and debrief occurring in the same week and every week, the year-long simulation course will have a simulation nearly every other Friday, and on the remaining available Fridays, the previous simulation will be debriefed (if not done after or during the simulation), and students will prepare for the simulation the following week.

Finally, when students participate in a semester or year-long simulation course, we encourage you to require them to keep a professional development journal. This meets a CAATE standard but also helps them to understand their current level and how to progress to the next. This is an additional method of reflective practice that students have benefited from in conjunction with simulation. Here, students are free to discuss actual patient care as well.

Glossary

HCP—Healthcare provider. In Clinical Simulations for the Athletic Trainer, we will mostly be referring to students as healthcare providers because that is what we are training them to be.

EP—Embedded personnel. This is a person involved in the simulation other than a SP. Examples of an EP could be a coach, EMS, or a parent.

prebrief—Information provided to simulation participants just prior to participation in the simulation. Information can be provided earlier, but it is essential information on the situation and other guidelines regarding the simulation.

preparation—Information or activities that require time but are essential or helpful in guiding the HCP to have a successful simulation.

standardized patient (SP)—An individual trained to portray a specific patient case consistently and reliably across multiple student encounters.
